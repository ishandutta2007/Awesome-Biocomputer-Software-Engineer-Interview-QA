# Topic 02: Signal Acquisition & Electrophysiology

## Overview
Multi-electrode arrays, local field potentials, spike recording, amplifier chain design, and the electrical signal characteristics a Biocomputer Software Engineer must understand to build effective acquisition pipelines.

---

### Q1: Describe the electrophysiology signal types recorded from biological neural network preparations (action potentials/spikes, local field potentials, multi-unit activity), and explain how each signal type's characteristics shape software acquisition pipeline design.

**A:**
**Action potentials (spikes, single-unit activity):** Brief (~1ms), large-amplitude (~100µV at the electrode, after amplification) voltage transients generated by individual neurons' membrane depolarization — the fundamental "digital-like" signaling unit of biological neural communication. Spike detection and sorting (Topic 05) is typically the primary signal type targeted for information extraction in neural decoding applications.
- Acquisition pipeline implications: Require relatively high sampling rates (typically 20-30 kHz or higher) to adequately characterize spike waveform shapes needed for spike sorting, and high-pass filtering (typically above ~300 Hz) to isolate the spike-frequency band from slower signal components.

**Local field potentials (LFPs):** Slower (Hz to hundreds of Hz range), spatially-averaged electrical potential fluctuations reflecting summed synaptic activity and synchronized network dynamics across a population of neurons within a local volume around the recording electrode.
- Acquisition pipeline implications: Require lower sampling rates (typically 1-2 kHz is adequate for the relevant frequency range) and low-pass filtering (typically below ~300 Hz), and carry different information content than individual spikes (population-level synchrony, oscillatory dynamics, network state) — often processed in parallel with but separately from the high-frequency spike band in multi-band acquisition architectures.

**Multi-unit activity (MUA):** The unsorted, aggregate spiking signal from multiple neurons near an electrode, without attempting to attribute individual spikes to specific source neurons — lower-information-content per recorded unit than fully-sorted single-unit data, but computationally less demanding and more robust in low-signal-quality recording conditions.
- Acquisition pipeline implications: Intermediate between single-unit and LFP processing, allowing simpler (threshold-based) spike detection without full spike sorting while still extracting useful population-level spiking rate information.

**Software pipeline architecture implication — multi-band, parallel processing:** Given the genuinely different signal characteristics, optimal filtering parameters, and downstream use cases of these different signal types (all present simultaneously in the raw electrical recording), a well-designed acquisition pipeline architecture typically performs parallel, simultaneous extraction of multiple signal types (spiking band and LFP band, at minimum) from a single raw data stream, rather than a single monolithic processing path that would inevitably compromise on parameters not simultaneously optimal for all signal types — connecting to the more general software architecture considerations discussed in Topic 06.

### Q2: What are the primary sources of noise and artifact in neural electrophysiology recordings, and how do software-based approaches complement hardware-based approaches in mitigating these?

**A:**
**Primary noise/artifact sources:**

1. **Thermal (Johnson) noise from electrode-electrolyte interface:** Fundamental, physics-limited noise floor determined by electrode impedance and temperature — predominantly a hardware design/electrode material consideration (lower-impedance electrodes, better materials), though software can help (e.g., averaging across multiple electrodes recording the same signal to improve SNR).

2. **Common-mode noise (electromagnetic interference, power line noise at 50/60 Hz and harmonics):** Electrical noise present coherently across all or many recording channels simultaneously, arising from external electromagnetic sources (power supplies, motors, nearby electronic equipment) — best addressed in hardware through differential recording (recording voltage differences between a signal electrode and a reference electrode, ideally experiencing similar common-mode interference) and physical shielding, but with complementary software-based approaches including: common-average referencing (subtracting the mean signal across all channels from each channel, effectively re-referencing to cancel coherent common-mode noise), and digital notch filtering at specific known interference frequencies (though with care to avoid distorting signals of interest near the notch frequency).

3. **Stimulation artifacts:** Large, brief electrical transients introduced into the recording amplifier chain when electrical stimulation pulses are delivered to the biological substrate via stimulation electrodes — a critical software challenge for closed-loop systems (Topic 04) where stimulation and recording must occur in close temporal proximity, requiring software-based artifact detection, blanking (masking the signal during known artifact periods), and/or more sophisticated artifact template subtraction methods to enable useful recording close to stimulation events.

4. **Motion/mechanical artifacts:** Slow, large-amplitude baseline drifts or transient deflections caused by mechanical disturbance of the electrode-tissue interface — software approaches include high-pass filtering to remove slow baseline drift components, and artifact detection/rejection algorithms identifying and flagging epochs with motion-artifact signatures for exclusion from downstream analysis.

5. **Biological noise/activity:** Neural signals not specifically of interest for the current task (e.g., spiking from neurons the investigator isn't specifically targeting, network-level oscillations unrelated to the computational task) — "addressed" through appropriate experimental design and region-/cell-type-targeted electrode placement, rather than post-hoc software filtering that risks removing genuine signals of interest alongside "noise."

**Key software/hardware complementarity principle:** Hardware solutions (electrode material/geometry, differential amplification, physical shielding) address noise at its source and during the initial amplification stage, and are generally preferable where applicable because they don't alter or distort the signal before digitization — software-based noise mitigation methods act on already-digitized signals and are best applied complementarily for noise sources hardware approaches can't fully eliminate, with careful attention to avoiding signal distortion from overly aggressive software filtering.

## Summary
Neural electrophysiology signal acquisition requires matched multi-band parallel processing architectures (spike band, LFP band), careful noise source characterization and combined hardware-software mitigation strategies, and deep familiarity with stimulation artifact handling for closed-loop applications — the foundational signal-level competency underlying all higher-level biocomputer software functions.
